Understanding why impermanence is connected with suffering while recognizing pleasant, painful, and neutral feelings. These texts clarify what is being examined when a changing experience is regarded as mine, me, or my self.

## What each discourse contributes

- [[Sutta texts/SN 22.1#^healthy-in-mind|SN 22.1 — Nakula’s Father]] — Shows how changing aggregates can be experienced without the anguish produced by self-identification, beginning with the training for a healthy mind despite bodily illness.

- [[Sutta texts/SN 36.11#^impermanence-and-suffering|SN 36.11 — In Private]] — Directly explains the statement that suffering includes whatever is felt: it refers to the impermanence of conditions and their liability to end, fade, and cease. The distinction between pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling remains.

- [[Sutta texts/MN 44#^feeling-and-change|MN 44 — The Shorter Elaboration]] — Explains that pleasant feeling is pleasant while it remains and painful when it perishes. It also distinguishes the tendencies associated with feelings and how they are abandoned.

- [[Sutta texts/SN 18.5#Text|SN 18.5 — Feeling]] — Applies the recurring insight examination to feelings born of contact at each of the six senses.

- [[Sutta texts/MN 10#2. Observing the Feelings|MN 10 — Mindfulness Meditation]] — Gives the direct practice of knowing pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling, and contemplating its arising and passing.

## How this fits the collection

[[Sutta texts/SN 36.11#Text|SN 36.11 — In Private]] supplies context for the impermanence-and-suffering question in [[Practices/Insight framework (SN 18)|Insight framework (SN 18)]]. [[Sutta texts/MN 44#Text|MN 44 — The Shorter Elaboration]] helps keep the immediate pleasantness of a feeling distinct from its changing condition. [[Practices/Joy and settling the mind|Joy and settling the mind]] shows how joy, rapture, and bliss can support meditation within that same understanding.
